AI & Energy

Beyond the Headlines

Jane Zhang’s independent exploration of AI and energy infrastructure, a developing study and a planned monthly Monitor comparing the United States, China and European Union.

Independent Exploration · Research in Progress

Artificial intelligence is advancing rapidly, with some describing its potential as a new industrial revolution. But technological progress does not happen in isolation. Behind it lies the physical infrastructure that makes it possible.

Energy is one of its most fundamental foundations.

If AI is going to transform our world, what kind of future are we building to support it?

Will we pursue technological and economic growth at the expense of our environment, repeating patterns from earlier industrial revolutions? Or can we find a more balanced path—one that considers reliability, affordability, environmental responsibility and long-term societal needs?

These questions led me to begin exploring the relationship between AI and energy systems.

01 — Why I Started Exploring This

My starting point was not simply how much electricity artificial intelligence would consume. It was a broader question about the infrastructure we are choosing to build for its future.

Rapid AI development brings growing demands for data centres, electricity generation, transmission networks and other supporting infrastructure.

But energy infrastructure is not something we build for today alone.

Decisions about power generation, grid expansion and large industrial investments can influence energy costs, emissions, communities and the environment for decades.

I began wondering whether the urgency to support AI development might lead us towards short-term solutions with lasting consequences. Equally, could this moment offer an opportunity to strengthen our energy systems in ways that benefit society beyond the AI industry itself?

I wanted to understand the possibilities and trade-offs rather than assume that one development pathway was necessarily better than another.

That curiosity gradually became a more systematic exploration of electricity systems, infrastructure planning, regulation, investment and the different approaches being taken around the world.

02 — Questions I’m Investigating

My exploration currently centres on several interconnected questions:

  • How can electricity systems integrate rapidly growing data-centre loads while maintaining reliability?
  • What roles should grid reinforcement, new generation, storage and demand flexibility play?
  • Under what conditions are shared infrastructure solutions preferable to more individualised approaches?
  • How should infrastructure costs, benefits and long-term risks be allocated?
  • How do different development pathways affect electricity affordability, emissions and wider communities?

These are questions I am investigating, not conclusions I have already reached.

03 — An Independent Study in Progress

I am developing a study examining alternative approaches to integrating large electricity loads, particularly data centres, into energy systems.

One potential comparison concerns shared grid infrastructure and more individualised electricity-supply arrangements.

My aim is to understand the conditions under which different approaches may perform better, considering reliability, infrastructure investment, costs, flexibility and environmental implications.

The research question, methodology and evaluation framework are still evolving as I review existing literature and available evidence.

I will share the study when its methods and findings are sufficiently developed to support a meaningful contribution.

04 — AI & Energy Monitor

Following three regions, understanding different directions

Alongside my independent study, I am developing a monthly series examining significant AI–energy developments across the United States, China and the European Union.

Rather than compiling every news story, the monitor will focus on developments that help explain how these regions are responding to growing electricity demand and what directions their energy systems may be taking.

Each monthly article will consider:

  • What changed: Important developments in AI electricity demand, generation, grid infrastructure, flexibility, policy and investment.
  • How the regions compare: Where priorities, strategies and constraints converge or differ.
  • Why it matters: The possible implications for electricity systems, costs, reliability and the environment.
  • What to watch: Unresolved questions, future milestones and evidence that may confirm or challenge emerging trends.

The aim is to build a dated, source-supported record that makes developments easier to follow and compare over time.

05 — How I Approach the Work

I believe meaningful analysis requires distinguishing between what is known, what is proposed and what remains uncertain.

In this work, I aim to use identifiable sources, examine underlying assumptions and separate documented developments from my own interpretation.

Announcements, regulatory approvals, construction and operational results are not interchangeable. Nor do the United States, China and the European Union operate under identical institutional conditions.

My understanding will continue to evolve as new evidence becomes available.

This space is where I intend to share that process: following developments, questioning assumptions and gradually building a clearer understanding of the energy systems supporting the AI era.
