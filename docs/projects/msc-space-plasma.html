Physics-Informed Machine Learning in Space Plasma Physics

Exploring the evolving solar wind through scientific machine learning.

01 — The Research Question

Can machine learning help us understand how the solar wind evolves?

The solar wind is a continuous flow of charged particles from the Sun. Its behaviour is shaped by complex interactions involving particle motion, magnetic fields and changing plasma conditions.

One way to investigate these processes is through velocity distribution functions (VDFs), which describe how particles are distributed across different velocities. They offer a connection between microscopic particle behaviour and larger-scale plasma dynamics.

However, real spacecraft observations are challenging to analyse. Measurements from different instruments must be carefully aligned, particle populations frequently overlap, and the physical environment changes continuously.

My MSc research explored whether combining machine learning with physical knowledge could help investigate these evolving structures.

Rather than identifying patterns in isolated observations, I wanted to understand how particle populations, broader plasma conditions and their dynamical relationships might be studied together.

02 — How I Investigated It

Connecting data, physical knowledge and complementary models

I worked with observations from ESA’s Solar Orbiter mission, focusing on a selected interval from 14 July 2020, when the spacecraft was approximately 0.64 astronomical units from the Sun.

A substantial part of the work involved preparing the observational data: examining measurement quality, aligning datasets with different sampling characteristics, and engineering features that reflected the underlying physics.

I then combined three machine-learning approaches:

  • Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) to investigate particle populations within velocity distributions and examine their changes over time.
  • HDBSCAN to identify broader plasma regimes without imposing fixed cluster shapes or forcing every observation into a group.
  • Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) to examine interpretable relationships between evolving plasma variables and provide another perspective on the clustering results.

Physical knowledge informed the analytical process, from feature construction and model selection to evaluation and interpretation.

The objective was not simply to produce statistically distinct clusters, but to investigate whether the identified patterns could be understood within the physical context of the solar wind.

03 — What I Found

Structure within an evolving plasma environment

The original dissertation produced several findings.

GMM analysis identified four distinguishable particle populations, with different velocity characteristics and temporal behaviour. Their evolution suggested possible differences in how populations respond to changing plasma conditions. However, identifying these components with specific ion species required further validation.

At the macroscopic level, HDBSCAN identified three plasma regimes, with approximately 32% of observations classified as noise. These results suggested that the selected interval contained complex and transitional plasma conditions rather than sharply separated, static categories.

SINDy provided an additional view of the dynamical relationships within these regimes, helping to interpret the clustering outcomes in relation to known physical behaviour.

Together, the results demonstrated how complementary models could reveal different aspects of the same evolving system.

The investigation also generated further hypotheses about particle-population evolution. I regarded these as questions for future research, not established discoveries.

04 — Revisiting the Research

From research notebooks to reusable software

After completing my dissertation, I returned to the project with another objective: to understand how experimental research code could be developed into more structured, maintainable and reproducible scientific software.

I reorganised the original notebook-based work into Python modules with separate responsibilities for data preprocessing, modelling and scientific analysis. I also introduced automated tests, dependency management and documentation.

This gave me practical experience with modular software design while allowing the research to continue developing.

Questioning the original feature engineering

Revisiting the project also prompted me to examine some earlier modelling decisions.

During this review, I recognised that certain engineered features used in the original clustering analysis were not well suited to the physical question being investigated. One example was the inclusion of magnetic-field components divided by Alfvén speed, a transformation whose physical meaning and potential redundancy required reconsideration.

The revised analysis no longer used those features and identified two dominant plasma sub-regimes, rather than the three reported in the original dissertation.

The extended investigation also incorporated additional kinetic diagnostics and regime-aware SINDy analysis.

Because the later analysis involved revised modelling choices, the different clustering outcomes should not be attributed to one feature change without a controlled comparison.

For me, this was an important reminder that analytical results must remain open to reassessment. A model can produce convincing statistical patterns even when aspects of the underlying feature representation deserve further scrutiny.

05 — Limitations, Reflections and Resources

What the project taught me

This research reinforced two lessons.

The first concerns scientific reasoning. Machine-learning results become more meaningful when the data, assumptions and evaluation methods are understood within their domain. Statistical patterns alone do not establish physical explanations.

The second concerns technical practice. Moving from exploratory notebooks to reusable modules helped me understand the importance of separating responsibilities, documenting dependencies, introducing tests and making analytical work easier to examine and extend.

Important limitations remain. The particle identities require stronger physical validation, the clustering results warrant further robustness testing, and the findings are based on a limited observational interval.

I see the project as both a completed academic investigation and an evolving learning experience: one that strengthened my interest in scientific machine learning, interpretable methods and the careful development of data-driven research.
